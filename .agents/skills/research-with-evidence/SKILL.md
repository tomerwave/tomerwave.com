# Research With Evidence

Source: godharness-authored.

## When to use it

Before recommending a library, tool, technique, or approach based on claims found online or in
documentation — anywhere a decision would otherwise rest on an unverified search-result summary
rather than a checked source.

## The playbook

1. **Search broadly first, then verify narrowly.** Treat a search engine's summary as a lead, not
   an answer — fetch the actual source before citing what it says.
2. **Confirm every link you plan to cite actually resolves and actually supports the specific
   claim it's attached to.** A link that 404s, redirects somewhere unrelated, or supports a
   weaker/different claim than the one made is worse than no citation — it manufactures false
   confidence.
3. **For each option under consideration, record the specific evidence for it and against it** —
   not a vibe or a popularity signal, but what a fetched source actually says, including version
   numbers, benchmark figures, or maintenance status where relevant.
4. **Write the result as a concise doc**: the question, each option considered, the evidence for
   and against each, and a proposal — not a narrated transcript of the research process.
5. **Say plainly when evidence is missing or conflicting**, rather than picking a confident-sounding
   answer to fill the gap. "I didn't find reliable evidence either way" is a valid, useful finding.

## Why this matters

An unverified claim repeated from a search snippet can be stale, wrong, or describing a different
version of the thing than the one being evaluated — and it looks identical to a verified one
until someone acts on it and it fails. Checking links and recording the actual evidence per
option is what makes a research doc something a reader can trust and act on directly, instead of
something that needs to be re-verified before it's useful.
